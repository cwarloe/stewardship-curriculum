# R001 — What research actually applies to this seminar

**Status:** assessed · **Date:** 2026-09-29 · **Informs:** ADR-001, ADR-002, and the open timeline decision

**Question:** For a 5–10 person seminar (online kickoff → home work → weekend → follow-up) that aims to change how people steward time, money, and gifts, which bodies of research apply, what do they say, and what timeline do they imply?

## Provenance — read this first

- Searched 2026-09-29 by web search only. **Full-text fetching was blocked by this environment's network policy**, so no paper below was read in full. Every source is at the **found** or **abstract** level (see [PROTOCOL](PROTOCOL.md)). Figures come from abstracts and search-result summaries.
- The figures most worth checking against the full text are marked ⚑.
- Findings carried over from NADF were assessed there (NADF R005, R006, R008, and `skills/technical-training-design`), under the same limits. They're summarized here, not re-assessed.

## The headline

1. **The seminar's shape is supported. Its weakest point is the follow-up, not the weekend.** The research agrees that effects from one intensive event fade within weeks to months unless something reinforces them. The follow-up is the part that makes the weekend count.
2. **Teaching about money changes behavior less than people expect.** Plan the treasure session around doing (set something up, track it, report it) rather than explaining.
3. **Nothing was found that studies this kind of seminar directly.** Everything below is borrowed from adjacent fields and labeled that way.

## 1. Spacing: how far apart the kickoff and weekend should be

**Finding.** Across 317 experiments, spaced study beats massed study. The best gap between sessions grows with how long you need to remember (Cepeda et al., 2006). In a study of more than 1,350 people, the optimal gap was about **20% of the retention interval** for retention measured in weeks, falling to about **5%** for a one-year retention interval ⚑. Past the optimum, performance declined only *gradually*, while gaps that were too short cost more (Cepeda et al., 2008).

**Applied [DERIVED].** The seminar wants the weekend's content to still be alive about a year later. At about 5% of a year, the optimal gap is **roughly 2.5–3 weeks**. A separate constraint pushes it longer: a spending log needs about a month to mean anything (one pay cycle plus the irregular bills). Because going slightly past the optimum costs little, **4–5 weeks** is a reasonable compromise; less than about 2.5 weeks is the thing to avoid.

**Borrowing.** These studies measured recall of facts and vocabulary, not changed behavior or spiritual formation. Using them for seminar spacing is this project's extrapolation.

> Confidence: **High** that spacing beats massing. **Low–moderate** that the specific ratio transfers to a formation seminar.

## 2. Habit formation: how long the follow-up has to run

**Finding.** Ninety-six adults repeated one chosen daily behavior. Time to reach peak automaticity averaged **66 days**, but ranged from **18 to about 254 days** ⚑. Early repetitions produced the largest gains. The 66-day figure comes from the roughly half of participants whose data fit the model well (Lally et al., 2010).

**Finding.** Habits are cued by stable contexts. When a life change disrupts those cues (a move, a new job, marriage), behavior becomes open to deliberate choice for a while. This is the *habit discontinuity hypothesis* (Verplanken & Wood, 2006; Wood & Neal, 2007).

**Applied [DERIVED].**
- **The first two to three weeks after the weekend matter most.** Early repetitions carry the largest gains, so the heaviest follow-up support belongs there, not spread evenly.
- **Follow-up should run at least about 10–12 weeks**, past the 66-day average, with an explicit expectation that some people need much longer.
- **Commitments should be daily or weekly behaviors in a stable context** ("after Sunday lunch, I review the week's spending"), not general intentions.
- **[OPTION]** People in life transitions (a new job, a move, marriage, retirement) may be the most receptive participants. This is a recruiting question, not yet a decision.

> Confidence: **Moderate.** One well-known study with wide individual variation and a small, young sample. The discontinuity work is robust but mostly concerns travel and consumer habits.

## 3. Making commitments that survive the trip home

**Finding.** *Implementation intentions* are if-then plans ("when X happens, I will do Y"). Across 94 tests and more than 8,000 people, they improved goal attainment over a goal alone, with **d ≈ 0.65** ⚑ (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

**Finding.** Monitoring goal progress improves attainment, **d ≈ 0.40** across 138 trials (about 19,900 people), including money goals. The effect was **stronger when progress was reported to others and physically recorded** (Harkin et al., 2016).

**Finding.** In a meta-analysis of 89 studies of workplace training transfer, most predictors (motivation, a supportive environment) mattered **more for open skills** like leadership than for closed skills like software. How and when transfer is measured changes the results (Blume et al., 2010). Stewardship is an open skill, so the environment people return to matters more, not less.

**Finding.** Across 40 studies, follow-up "boosters" after an intervention ended (mostly phone calls and texts) added a **modest** sustained effect on physical activity (≈6% over the intervention alone ⚑) (Tarantino et al., 2025). A separate review found no benefit from boosters in chronic pain self-management, so the effect isn't universal.

**Applied [DERIVED].**
- **Saturday ends with written if-then plans**, not a general "decision" or a vague commitment. This is the strongest single mechanism in this record.
- **The follow-up asks people to record and report**, to a partner, spouse, or the group. A private reflection prompt is the weaker form.
- **Couples and partners are part of the environment people go home to.** Blume's open-skill finding says this support matters. Pairing people for follow-up is supported, even though no study tested it in this setting.
- **For the single-men pilot (ADR-005):** there's no spouse at home, so the group is the support environment. Pair the men with each other for the follow-up, and set the pairs before the weekend ends.
- **Keep boosters light and early** (a call or message). Expect a modest effect, not a rescue.

> Confidence: **Moderate–High** for implementation intentions and monitoring, both large meta-analyses across domains. **Moderate** for training transfer and boosters.

## 4. Group size and discussion

**Finding.** In an experiment comparing 5-person and 10-person groups, **5-person discussions worked like dialogue**: members were most influenced by the people they actually talked with. **10-person discussions worked like serial monologue**: members were most influenced by the dominant speaker (Fay, Garrod & Carletta, 2000).

**Finding.** Practitioner guidance for focus groups converges on 5–8 participants. That's convention, not experimental evidence.

**Applied [DERIVED].**
- **5–8 can run as one discussion group.** At 9–10, split into two groups for the discussion segments and bring everyone together for teaching and closing.
- **Plan for the dominant speaker at any size**: go around the circle, write before speaking, have pairs report back. The larger the group, the more this matters.
- **Later runs, not the pilot:** couples are an unstudied variable here. Spouses may speak for each other or defer. Consider splitting couples across sub-groups for some discussions and keeping them together for the treasure planning. `[OPTION]`

> Confidence: **Moderate.** One strong experiment, not replicated in this search, and a lab task rather than a formation conversation.

## 5. Money: what teaching can and can't do

**Finding, conflicting.**
- A meta-analysis of 201 studies found financial-literacy interventions explained about **0.1% of the variance** in financial behavior. Effects were weaker in low-income samples and **negligible about 20 months later** even after many hours of instruction (Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer, 2014).
- A later meta-analysis limited to **76 randomized experiments** (about 160,000 people) found positive effects on knowledge and behavior, **"similar to those realized by educational interventions in other domains"** and at least three times larger than the earlier estimate (Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban, 2022). Kaiser & Menkhoff (2017) found effects were larger when education came at a **"teachable moment"**, right before the relevant decision.

**Reading the conflict.** The two papers don't disagree about decay. Both are consistent with **modest, fading effects of education alone**, and more effect when learning happens close to the action.

**Finding, descriptive baseline.** Among U.S. Christians, about 22% give nothing in a given year, most churchgoers give 2–3% of income, and about 9% give 10% or more (Smith & Emerson, 2008, *Passing the Plate*). This is survey data, useful for context, not an intervention study.

**Applied [DERIVED].**
- **The treasure session is where participants act, not where they learn facts.** For example, set up the recurring gift, write the budget line, or schedule the monthly review in the room. That makes the weekend the teachable moment.
- **The pre-work spending log matters more than the teaching.** It gives people their own data before the weekend.
- **Don't promise a change in giving.** The evidence doesn't support expecting one from a weekend alone.

> Confidence: **Moderate** that education alone produces modest, fading financial behavior change. **Low** on how any of this transfers to religious giving, which has different motives.

## 6. Retreats, faith formation, and stewardship programs

**Finding: essentially nothing direct.**
- Retreat research is mostly about **meditation retreats**. One Vipassana study found psychological gains maintained at a 4-week follow-up. It's a different practice and population, so the transfer is weak.
- Stewardship program literature found in this search is **practitioner and case-study material** (denominational guides, a D.Min. project, a six-year United Church of Canada congregational study) rather than outcome evaluations.
- Wuthnow's small-groups study (*Sharing the Journey*, 1994) documents that religious small groups are common and valued. It's descriptive and says nothing about which designs change behavior.

**This is the expected result, not a failed search.** NADF hit the same wall for analyst training. The design rests on the borrowed evidence above, and the seminar's own runs will be the first evidence for this format.

> Confidence: **Moderate** that no controlled evidence exists. A search in ATLA or religious-education journals could still find something, and that search is worth doing before the second run.

## 7. Carried over from NADF (assessed there)

These apply as they are, within the same limits:

| Finding | Design use here |
|---|---|
| Retrieval with feedback: *predict then check* is the strongest form that works without a second person | Before the time log or spending log, people write down what they expect to find, then compare |
| Distributed practice beats massed practice | Revisit the core ideas (ownership, faithfulness in little) in each phase, not only once |
| Seductive details reduce learning while raising ratings | The case study and every illustration must carry the point, or it comes out |
| Three rungs: worked example → guided practice → the person's own work | Case study → group discussion using their own data → their own plan |
| Effects from a single sitting fade within weeks without reinforcement | Confirms §2–3: the follow-up is essential |
| A verification tracker (decision, confidence, horizon, revisit) | The commitment log: plan, how confident, a check date, the result |
| With small n, report overlap descriptively and decline to compute statistics | For measuring whether it worked: describe, don't score |

What **doesn't** carry over from NADF: the NICE role mapping (R007), packet-capture sourcing (R004), and the reviewer-agreement statistics (R009). This seminar grades nobody.

## Timeline [DERIVED] — adopted as ADR-006

Every number traces to a section above. **The follow-up is five contacts over 12 weeks, not a weekly meeting.** The weekly part is each man's own chosen behavior, which runs on his schedule. The program adds three short check-ins early, when they matter most (§2), and two group touchpoints later. For how the mindset shift fits into this sequence, see [R002](R002-mindset-before-behavior.md).

| When | What | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Week 0 | Online kickoff (~90 min). Each person predicts where his time, money, and gifts go, then starts the logs | §1, §7 predict-then-check |
| Weeks 0–4 | Time log (one ordinary week), spending log (a full month), gifts inventory including two outside views | §1 (about a month of data), §5 (own data before teaching) |
| Weeks 4–5 | Weekend. Friday evening: foundation. Saturday: time, treasure, talents, closing. **Saturday ends with written if-then plans** | §1 (optimum about 2.5–3 weeks; a month of spending data pushes it to 4–5), §3 implementation intentions |
| Weekend + 1, 2, 3 weeks | Short check-ins (~10 minutes, with his pair partner or by message) with progress **recorded and reported** | §2 (early repetitions matter most), §3 monitoring and boosters |
| Weekend + ~6 and ~12 weeks | Group touchpoints; re-run a shortened time and spending log and compare with the pre-work | §2 (past the 66-day average), §7 verification tracker |
| Weekend + ~6–12 months | `[OPTION]` Reunion or re-log | §5 (decay at ~20 months); nothing sets this date precisely |

## What would tell us it isn't working

Decide this before the first run (NADF R009's lesson):

- **The re-logged week and month look like the pre-work.** No change in where time and money go.
- **Fewer than half of the if-then plans are still being carried out at the 6-week check-in**, by the person's own record. The threshold is a judgment call; there's no published benchmark for this setting.
- **Check-ins stop happening.** If the follow-up doesn't run, §2–3 say the weekend's effect won't last.

With 5–10 people, report these descriptively. Don't compute statistics.

## What this does not establish

- That this seminar format works. There is no outcome data for it anywhere, and the first runs will produce the only evidence.
- Anything about spiritual formation as such. The borrowed research measures behavior, recall, and habits. Whether stewardship is being formed in the heart is a question the author answers theologically, not this record.
- Exact figures. Nothing was read in full, and the ⚑ items should be checked before anyone quotes them.
